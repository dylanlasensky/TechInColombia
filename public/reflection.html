Final reflection

What I’m taking away

How my view of Colombia changed

I started this project with a topic that already connected to my life. I study Computer Science and work in cybersecurity. I wanted to understand how technology affects everyday life in Colombia. At first, I was mostly thinking about internet access and digital services. I still care about those things. Now I pay more attention to who gets to use them and who gets to shape them.

My understanding of Colombia became more specific as I built the portfolio. A student in Medellín and a family in a rural community cannot stand in for the whole country. The sources cover different places and different years. Recognizing those limits helped me take regional diversity more seriously. I also began to see Colombians as people creating their own digital tools and stories. That feels more accurate than treating the country as a place simply waiting for new technology.

The finding that surprised me most

The internet subsidy study by Hidalgo and Sovinsky influenced my thinking the most. Their model suggested that many existing customers who switched plans chose slower service. I would have expected a subsidy to make a better connection easier to afford. The study made me separate a lower bill from a more useful connection. Those are related goals but they are not the same outcome.

That changed the questions I would ask about a technology project. I would want to know whether someone could actually finish a class assignment or use a service reliably. Counting connections would only tell part of the story. The study uses data from 2013 and 2014. I cannot use it to describe Colombia’s internet today. Its biggest lesson for me is about checking assumptions. A policy can sound helpful without working exactly how I expect. That is a useful reminder for someone who wants to work on the business side of tech.

The artifact that stayed with me

The most meaningful artifact for me was Reconstrucción. This Colombian graphic adventure uses digital storytelling to engage with the armed conflict. Its project materials gave me a different way to connect computing with Colombian culture. Software does not only process information or make a task faster. It can also shape how someone encounters a story about the past.

This connects to the course themes of memory and peacebuilding. A game has to make choices about whose perspective appears and what a player can do. Those choices matter because they influence how people understand an experience. I cannot say that playing a game creates reconciliation. It also cannot replace listening to people who lived through violence. What stayed with me was the responsibility involved in representing that history. As a Computer Science student, I need to think about the meaning of a design as well as whether it works.

How the portfolio came together

The research and artifacts became more useful when I stopped looking at them separately. The study of home technology and Saber 11 results found that benefits varied across subjects. The photograph of laptop delivery in Tumaco shows a moment of access. Together they helped me see why a photograph of new equipment cannot tell the whole story of student learning. I would still need to ask about teaching and continued support.

My interdisciplinary connections helped organize those questions. Education asks how students learn. Economics asks what people can afford. Political science asks how institutions make decisions and take responsibility. The Radio Sutatenza newsreel adds representation to that discussion. It presents technology through a government message about progress. Comparing these pieces taught me to ask what a source shows and what it leaves out. That made the portfolio more than a collection of positive examples.

What this means for my path

The cybersecurity research connected especially well to my current work. Díaz Acevedo and Cremades Guisado described technical progress moving faster than organizational coordination. That reinforced something I want to carry forward as an analyst. A security recommendation needs people who understand their responsibilities. Explaining a risk clearly can matter as much as identifying it.

My plans for a STEM designated MBA at Crummer give this another connection. I want to understand business decisions without losing the technical side of my background. The Nequi advertisement helped me think about how a company presents a product as useful in ordinary working life. The research on smaller Colombian businesses adds the question of whether a business is ready to use it. I would want to evaluate training and ongoing support alongside the product itself. The project pushed me to think about success from the user’s side.

What I would explore next

I would like to compare how small businesses in Armenia and Medellín use digital payments. I would ask owners what makes a service worth using and what security concerns they have. I would also ask how unreliable internet affects their work. This would let me investigate regional differences instead of assuming what they are.

I would want to include businesses that stopped using a service. Their experiences could show problems that success stories miss. That question brings together my interests in computing and business. My main takeaway is that useful technology starts with understanding people’s circumstances. I want to keep asking who benefits and how I would know. Colombia gave me concrete reasons to ask those questions more carefully.

Dylan Lasensky / Colombian Culture in Motion / Fall 2026
